What Five Agents, Three Tickets and $3,248 of Approved Payments Taught Us About Building an Agent Team

Philipp

Homework 5 · Problem 10: Reflection · Campus Customs back-office agent team

October 10, 2026

Abstract

In short. The team reached the correct result on all three tickets. It never moved a dollar without a human, never paid twice and never leaked an internal figure to a customer. It was also expensive, slow and not fully repeatable. The safety we are proudest of came from the tools and the backend, not from the number of agents. The costs we would cut came mostly from the agents talking to each other. My own view, argued in sections 3 and 7, is that these three tickets were isolated tasks. As the shop adds tools and workflows, the team's specialization becomes a clear advantage over a single agent. Every number on this page comes from the three final runs in output/audit_trail.json, the working database and the code. Where we have no measurement, we say so.

1 Judging the team: what counts as performance

An agent team can look impressive in a demo and still be a poor employee. We therefore judged it the way the owner of Campus Customs would judge a new hire, on five questions. Was the result correct against the database? Was it safe, meaning no money moved without a person, nothing was paid twice and nothing internal reached a customer? What did it cost in tokens and time? Would it do the same thing again? And did every member of the team actually contribute? The first two are the minimum standard. A back-office assistant that is fast but occasionally pays an invoice twice is worse than no assistant at all. The last three decide whether the design is worth keeping.

Ticket 101: correct, careful and costly

Ticket 101 was the hardest test, and the team passed it on substance. Inventory's check_order_fulfillment found what a busy shop assistant might have missed. Size S was not merely out of stock: the reprint supplier, Bulldog Print Co, would not ship because invoice 501 was three days overdue. The Boss authorized exactly $840.00, Accounting submitted it, and the run waited on the approval board until Philipp reviewed and approved the payment. The run then continued: the Boss ordered exactly one $8.00 shirt, which again waited for Philipp, and Customer service gave Tauhid the date the data supports, 2026-08-31 plus five lead days, which is 2026-09-05, with the sizes in stock as an alternative. All of this happened in one run, with nothing paid twice.

The cost tells a less flattering story. This one $28.00 tee consumed 93,355 tokens, 49% of everything the team used, in 82 seconds of team work (the run also waited 4 minutes for Philipp). Much of that is coordination. Accounting was called three times: once to analyse, once only to carry the Boss's payment authorization, when it re-read the cash position it had just read, and once to order the shirt, when it re-read the ticket. The Boss read the snapshot and the ticket twice each. Four of the run's 16 tool calls repeated an identical call. Finally, the ticket cannot be closed by anything we built, because no tool records the delivery. We count 101 as a partial success: the right decisions at a high price, with an ending the system cannot reach on its own.

Ticket 102: the design at its best

Ticket 102 is the ticket we would show an investor. Facilities answered the key question, whether the notice is genuine, in a single call to review_rent_payment: the requester is the landlord, $2,400.00 is the monthly rent, the due date is 2026-09-02, and the shop would keep $152.00 after paying. The Boss authorized the exact rent and Philipp approved it. The payment then updated cash, the payments table and the lease's next due date in one transaction, as the shop rules demand. The run waited for Philipp's decision and then closed the ticket itself: one run, 48 seconds of team work, 46,432 tokens. The remaining weakness is the briefing: the Boss used 28,612 of those tokens, more than four times the 6,751 Facilities needed to answer the key question.

Ticket 103: efficient, safe and not reproducible

Ticket 103 was the cheapest ticket per run of work: one run, nine tool calls, 46 seconds. That was because evaluate_bulk_discount_request gave Accounting everything at once: the 8 hoodies in stock, the margin at each discount, the Boss's 15% authority and the $264.00 cost of restocking the other 12. The Boss rightly skipped Inventory and rightly offered only the 8, because $264.00 was more than the $152.00 left after the rent. The arithmetic is correct: $58.00 × 0.85 = $49.30 per hoodie, a 55.4% margin, $394.40 for the eight. The problem is the decision itself.

2 Plan versus reality

Before any agent ran, we wrote down how we expected each ticket to unfold. Those plans are kept unchanged in the yellow boxes of the ticket tabs, and comparing them with the green Actual sections is the most honest test of our understanding.

Where the plan held

  • The order of specialists was right on all three tickets: Inventory then Accounting for 101, Facilities first for 102, Accounting for the discount on 103.
  • The human gate sat exactly where we expected it, in front of every payment.
  • Each ticket was handled in one pass: the team paused at every approval and continued once the human had decided.
  • Customer service drafted the replies, and the guardrails kept them clean.

Where reality differed

  • 101: paying the invoice did not "release the shirts", because none were on order. A purchase order and a second approval were needed.
  • 102 and 103: combined tools replaced the steps we planned (review_rent_payment instead of get_lease_status; no check_stock at all).
  • 101: Inventory and Accounting worked in parallel, not one after the other.

The plan held in four respects. The order of specialists was right on all three tickets: Inventory and then Accounting for 101, Facilities first for 102, and Accounting for the discount on 103. The human gate sat exactly where we expected it, in front of every payment. Each ticket was handled in one pass: the team paused at every approval and continued once the human had decided. And Customer service drafted the replies, with the guardrails keeping them clean. Reality differed in three respects. On 101, paying the invoice did not "release the shirts", because none were on order, so a purchase order and a second approval were needed. On 102 and 103, combined tools replaced the steps we had planned (review_rent_payment instead of get_lease_status, and no check_stock at all). And on 101, Inventory and Accounting worked in parallel rather than one after the other.

Three lessons follow. First, our plans got the agents right and the data wrong. The biggest surprise, on 101, came from assuming an order existed. It did not, and the tools said so. A plan for an agent team is only as good as its picture of the database, so it should be written from the tool outputs rather than from the ticket text. Second, tool design changed the workflow more than our plan did: a single well-built tool replaced several planned steps on 102 and an entire agent on 103. Third, a human approval pauses the work, it does not end it. The team waits at the board for the decision and then continues with the result, so 101, with two approvals, still needed only one run, and no step had to be worked out a second time. None of the deviations made an outcome worse. Several made it better. The price was predictability: the same ticket did not always take the same path.

Across the three final runs, the Boss alone used 111,659 of the 190,452 tokens, or 59%; the four specialists together used the remaining 41%. Ninety-five percent of all tokens were input, because each model step re-reads its instructions, the tool descriptions and the conversation so far. The MCP tools themselves ran for only 3.7 seconds in total; the other 98% of the 175 seconds of team time was spent waiting for the model. The runs also waited 4 minutes 45 seconds for Philipp's decisions.

The imbalance is striking on the simpler tickets. On 102 Facilities needed two tool calls and 6,751 tokens, while the Boss spent 28,612, more than four times as much, much of it briefing Facilities and Accounting and reading their answers back. On 103 Accounting needed a single call and 7,856 tokens, against the Boss's 35,670. Across the three runs, 6 of the 34 agent tool calls repeated an identical call already made in the same run, mostly the Boss re-reading the shop snapshot and the ticket. The Boss did this even though its own instructions tell it to fetch a ticket only for details the snapshot lacks. A read-and-decide ticket such as 102 or 103 has one combined lookup, one decision, one record and one human gate. It very probably did not need five agents. We are careful here: we never ran a single-agent version, so "a single agent would be cheaper and faster" remains a well-founded hypothesis, not a result.

The case for the team rests on boundaries. Each agent sees only the tools its job needs: only Accounting can pay or order, and Customer service never sees a cost, a margin or the bank balance. Payments need two keys, the Boss's authorization and then a named human. That control worked in practice. During development Accounting once tried to pay invoice 501 without the Boss's authorization and was refused. And on 101 the parallel work of Inventory and Accounting genuinely saved time. But most of the protection we relied on lives in the shared tools: the overdraft check, the leak guard on drafts, the rule that a payment needs a human name, and the guard that stops a run from touching another ticket. A single agent connected to the same server would inherit every one of them. For these three tickets, the honest summary is that specialization earned its cost on the money path and in customer communication, and mostly added overhead elsewhere.

I think that summary nonetheless undersells the team, because our three tickets were relatively isolated, individual tasks, each needing only a handful of tools. A real shop would build many more tools and handle a far wider and more connected range of work: online orders, sales, returns, suppliers. The data already hints at why a team scales better under that growth. Since 95% of every model call is input, much of it the descriptions of the agent's tools, the number of tools an agent carries matters. Today each specialist carries between three and nine MCP tools (Facilities three, Accounting nine), while a single agent would carry all 23, plus every tool we add later, in every call. A team keeps each agent an expert in a small, well-defined job. It lets independent work run in parallel, as Inventory and Accounting did on 101. And a new capability can arrive as a new specialist without rewriting everyone else's instructions. So while a single agent might well perform comparably on these three tickets, I believe the agent team has a clear advantage in scalability, specialization and collaboration as the tasks and workflows grow more complex. This belief is informed by our data but not yet measured, and section 6 proposes how to test it.

4 Three new problems the team could solve today

Each scenario below uses only tools that exist and are tested. One honest caveat applies to all three: the system has no way to receive new work. Nothing in the app creates a ticket, so a person would first have to enter the request into the database. The scenarios start from the shop as the tickets left it, with invoice 501 paid, Bulldog Print Co unblocked and $152.00 in checking.

A partly fillable order. A customer orders five Classic Bulldog Tees in size L, and three are on the shelf. Inventory's check_order_fulfillment would report three on hand and two short, with the vendor now able to ship. ship_order_from_stock can send the three, because it allows partial quantities and logs each movement. Accounting would raise a $16.00 purchase order (2 × $8.00 cost) for the rest, which waits for a human. Once approved, checking would fall from $152.00 to $136.00. Customer service would then confirm what has shipped and when the rest is expected. The shipping rules are covered by tests but have never run live, and the missing two shirts would end up exactly where 101 is: waiting for a delivery the system cannot record.

A new supplier invoice blocks a restock. Suppose Elm City Gifts, the mug supplier, sends an overdue invoice while Crest Mugs are at zero. find_stock_shortfalls already flags the empty shelf and get_vendor_status the blocked vendor. From there the team would repeat the path it proved on 101: the Boss authorizes, a human approves pay_invoice, and Accounting orders new mugs at $3.50 each. If the invoice exceeded the cash on hand, the payment tool would refuse rather than overdraw, as the shop rules require. One weakness should be stated plainly: the database does not record which vendor supplies which product, so linking mugs to Elm City Gifts depends on the model reading the vendor's specialty.

A discount above the Boss's authority. A student group asks for 25% off ten size-L hoodies, and fourteen are in stock. The discount tool would show $43.50 per hoodie at a 49.4% margin, inside the safe range. Because 25% is above the Boss's 15%, record_discount_decision would go to the approval board instead of being recorded. Only after a person approved it would Inventory ship the ten and Customer service quote $435.00. This approval path is enforced in code and covered by unit tests, but no live run and no end-to-end API test has exercised it yet.

Next month's rent. The Cash tab ends with a warning: the next $2,400.00 rent is due on 2026-10-02 and the account holds $152.00. The team can see the problem but cannot act on it, because no tool can increase cash. Shipping eight hoodies would not record a sale, and the cash view only looks 30 days ahead at known bills. Excluding revenue was a rule of this homework, but in a real shop the gap would be fatal. The fix is three tools: record_sale, record_customer_payment and a forecast_cash that projects rent, invoices, orders and expected receipts. We would give forecasting to Accounting, but put incoming money with a new Sales agent, so that the agent that receives money is never the agent that pays it out. With these tools the Boss would see the shortfall weeks in advance and could plan collections, instead of discovering it on the due date.

The shirt arrives. Purchase order 1 is approved and due on 2026-09-05, but no tool can mark it received or put the shirt on the shelf. The shop's date is also a fixed value that never advances, so nothing would ever wake ticket 101. Tauhid will wait forever. The fix is a receive_purchase_order tool that adds the stock, records the movement and handles short or damaged deliveries. Receiving belongs to Inventory, so no new agent is needed. Alongside it we need a simple scheduler, not an agent, that starts a run when a ticket's expected date arrives. On 2026-09-05 Inventory would receive the shirt and ship it, Customer service would confirm, and the Boss could finally mark 101 resolved.

The customer answers. The Yale AI Club cannot accept the offer for eight hoodies inside this system. Starting a run takes no input, nothing creates or updates a ticket, and drafts are never sent. If the club asked for a different quantity, the recorded price could not be revised either. The fix is an ingest_message tool that attaches incoming messages to tickets, and a send_message that needs a human's approval, just as payments do. Routing incoming mail should start with plain rules (who sent it and which ticket it mentions) and only call a model when that is ambiguous. Our measurements suggest that another full agent for simple sorting would mostly add the overhead we saw in section 3. Customer service would then send the approved reply, and the club's "yes" would trigger the shipment and, with the Sales agent above, a recorded sale of $394.40.

6 What we learned

The safest parts of the system were the parts we wrote in code. The least reliable parts were the ones we left for the models to decide.

Looking across the three tickets, the pattern is consistent. Exact payment amounts, no overdrafts, no repeat payments, clean customer drafts and no writes outside the ticket being worked on held in every final run. Each of these is enforced by a tool or by the backend. What varied was everything we left to judgement: the discount percentage, which tools were called and how often, the number of drafts. At least three of the six bugs we found during development were fixed in tools rather than in prompts. A rule we left only in a prompt, fetching a ticket only for details the snapshot lacks, did not stop the Boss from fetching ticket 101 twice in one run.

If we rebuilt Campus Customs tomorrow, three changes would come first, in this order. First, put the discount policy in code: a tool that recommends a discount by quantity within the margin and authority limits, so that a customer's price no longer depends on the run. Second, cut the coordination overhead: let the Boss call the combined review tools directly, reuse identical reads within a run, and let a payment authorization join an existing delegation instead of requiring a new one. Third, ask for a reason on every approval: the board requires a name but not a note, and all three final approvals were saved without one, so nobody can later see why a payment was approved. A short required note would make every human decision auditable, and it would give the learning described in section 7 something to learn from. With 100 tickets instead of three, these changes become essential. Runs are processed one at a time, the Boss's snapshot grows with every open ticket, and each ticket plans its cash on its own. On today's numbers, one run per ticket at about 58 seconds of team time and 63,500 tokens, 100 tickets would mean at least an hour and a half of agent time, six million tokens, and a human answering every approval. That is an estimate, not a measurement.

The broader lesson is about when a team of agents is worth building. A multi-agent architecture earns its cost when the agents need different permissions (who may pay, who may talk to customers), when there is real separation of duties (the Boss decides, a human approves), or when work can run in parallel. Ticket 101 needed all three, and the team showed it. On a ticket that is simply "read one good tool, make one decision, write one record", as on 102 and 103, a single agent would probably do as well today. But a shop does not stay at three tickets, and the boundaries that made 101 safe become more valuable with every tool and workflow we add. Our next experiment should compare a single agent with the team twice: on these same three tickets, and on a larger, mixed workload with more tools. That would turn the argument into numbers.

7 Where we go from here

A team that learns. The feature I would most like to add is a learning capability, so that the more the agent team is used, the better it becomes. The raw material already exists: the audit trail records every tool call, every decision, every human approval and the reviewer's comment. Agents could learn which plans solved each type of ticket and skip the repeated lookups that made up 18% of our tool calls. They could also learn from past approvals and denials which proposals a human accepts, and from customer replies which offers win business. One rule would stay fixed: learning may improve the choices the agents make within the guardrails, but never relax the guardrails themselves. A learned pattern, such as a discount that customers reliably accept, should be reviewed by a human and then written into a tool, the same path we recommend for the discount policy above. That way the system grows more efficient without growing less safe.

A bigger team for a bigger shop. There is a lot of room to build on what we created. Integrating the team with the online shop would let orders arrive as tickets automatically, closing the intake gap described in section 4. Revenue tasks such as recording sales, collecting payments and forecasting cash would solve the rent problem in section 5. Further processes could also be automated: receiving deliveries, reordering low stock (find_stock_shortfalls already finds it), handling returns. Each of these is a new tool, and sometimes a new specialist, which is exactly the kind of growth the team structure is designed for.

Conclusion. Overall, I think this is a really strong starting point, and I am very happy with how everything turned out, especially how well the agents worked together. On ticket 101, four agents and a human coordinated in a single run to unblock a supplier, order a shirt and keep the customer informed, without a single wrong payment. On 102 they verified and paid the rent exactly once. On 103 they priced a bulk order safely and kept every internal number away from the customer. The project has reinforced my belief that an agent team is a great approach for this type of application, particularly as the complexity of the tasks grows. I genuinely believe that a real campus store could benefit from a system like this. I really enjoyed building it, and I am proud of the solution we developed.
